A shooter launches game pieces into a goal. The goal is not the highest speed, but the same shot every time.

## Principles

- **Consistency beats power.** Every piece should leave at the same speed and angle. Feed each piece the same way, and
  keep the flywheel at speed between shots.
- **Inertia.** A heavier flywheel (more moment of inertia) loses less speed when a piece goes through, so rapid shots
  stay consistent. It also takes longer to spin up. Pick the trade-off for your game.
- **Compression.** Like an intake, the wheel has to squeeze the piece to transfer energy. Prototype with real pieces
  to find the right gap.
- **Two-wheel shooters.** With wheels on both sides of the piece, gear or belt them together so they always run at the
  same speed ratio. Different speeds add spin on purpose.
- **Adjustable hood or angle.** Changing the exit angle lets the robot shoot from more places on the field.
- **Turrets.** A turret aims the shooter without turning the drivetrain, so the robot can shoot while moving or being
  defended. It adds weight and complexity.
- **Use a calculator.** [ReCalc's flywheel calculator](https://www.reca.lc/flywheel) estimates exit speed, recovery
  time, and spin-up time.

## Types

- **Single flywheel with hood:** One wheel pushes the piece against a curved hood. Good for balls.
- **Adjustable hood:** Moving the hood changes the exit angle, so the robot can shoot from more places.
- **Double flywheel:** Wheels on both sides of the piece. Good for discs, notes, and balls.
- **Turret:** The shooter rotates to aim, so the robot can shoot while driving.
- **Catapult or kicker:** Stored energy throws the piece in one stroke. Good for large balls.
