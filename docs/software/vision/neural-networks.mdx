# Deep Dive: How Neural Networks Work

Our [AprilTag detector](./detector-deep-dive.mdx) follows rules a person wrote down: find black and white
edges, look for four corners, read the bits. That works because an AprilTag was *designed* to be easy for
rules to find.

A game piece is different. It can be lit from any side, half hidden behind a robot, blurred because we're
driving past it, squished by a bumper, or sitting next to something the same color. Nobody can write rules
for every one of those cases. **Machine learning** takes a different approach: instead of writing the rules,
we show the computer thousands of examples with the right answers and let it work out the rules itself.

The tool that does this for pictures is a **neural network**. This page explains how one works, from a
single "neuron" up to the networks that find game pieces on our robot. You'll need some algebra and the idea
of a slope; there's no calculus you have to do yourself.

## One neuron

A neural network is built out of very simple pieces called **neurons** (named after brain cells, though they
work quite differently). A neuron takes in a few numbers and gives back one:

![A neuron: each input is multiplied by its weight, the results are added up with a bias, and the sum goes through an activation function](/img/software/vision/ml/1-neuron.webp)

1. Multiply each input $x_i$ by a **weight** $w_i$. A big weight means "this input matters a lot", a
   negative weight means "this input counts against".
2. Add them all up, plus one extra number called the **bias** $b$.
3. Pass the sum through an **activation function**. The most common one, **ReLU**, just replaces negative
   numbers with 0.

$$
\text{output} = \text{ReLU}\left(w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + w_3 x_3 + b\right),
\qquad
\text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z)
$$

For example, with inputs $(0.9, 0.2, 0.5)$, weights $(2.0, -1.0, 0.5)$ and bias $-0.6$: the sum is
$1.8 - 0.2 + 0.25 - 0.6 = 1.25$, which is positive, so the output is $1.25$.

On its own, a neuron isn't very smart: without the activation function it's just the equation of a line
(or a flat plane, with more inputs). The activation function is what bends it. Throw away everything below
zero, and lots of these bent pieces can be added together into any shape you like.

## A network: neurons in layers

A **neural network** is many neurons arranged in **layers**. The first layer takes the inputs (for a
picture, the brightness of every pixel). Each neuron in the next layer takes *all* the outputs of the layer
before it as its inputs, and so on, until the last layer gives the answer. Layers in between are called
**hidden layers**, and a network with many of them is a **deep** network, which is where the name
**deep learning** comes from.

All the network "knows" is stored in its weights and biases, called its **parameters**. The smallest YOLOv8
object detection model (YOLOv8 nano) has about **3 million** of them. Nobody sets those numbers by hand: they are
learned.

## Learning from examples

Here's a small network learning, trained right here for this page. It has two inputs, two hidden layers of
16 neurons and one output neuron, which uses a different activation (the **sigmoid**) to squeeze its answer into a
number from 0 to 1 meaning "how sure am I this dot is red". Each dot is one
training example, and the shaded background shows what the network would answer for every point:

![Four snapshots of a small network learning to separate red dots from blue dots. At the start its boundary is random; after 3000 steps it follows the curve between the two groups exactly.](/img/software/vision/ml/2-learning.webp)

At step 0 the weights are random numbers, so the network's answers are random too (60% right, barely better
than guessing). By the end, it has bent its boundary into exactly the curve between the two groups. Nobody
told it the shape. It found it by being corrected over and over. Here's how.

### 1. Measure how wrong it is: the loss

We need one number that says how wrong the network is on all the examples together. That number is the
**loss**. For "yes or no" answers like this one, we use **cross-entropy loss**: if the right answer is red
and the network says it's 90% sure of red, the loss for that example is small, $-\ln(0.9) \approx 0.11$. If
it says only 10%, the loss is big, $-\ln(0.1) \approx 2.3$. Being confidently wrong costs the most.

Training means **making the loss as small as possible** by changing the weights.

### 2. Walk downhill: gradient descent

Picture the loss as a landscape: every possible setting of the weights is a spot on the map, and the height
there is the loss. We want to reach the lowest valley.

We can't see the whole landscape (with 3 million weights, it has 3 million directions), but we can feel which
way the ground slopes right where we stand. That slope is the **gradient**: for each weight, "if I nudge this
weight up a tiny bit, does the loss go up or down, and how fast?" Then we take a small step downhill:

$$
w_{\text{new}} = w - \eta \cdot \frac{\partial\,\text{loss}}{\partial w}
$$

$\frac{\partial\,\text{loss}}{\partial w}$ is the slope of the loss for that one weight, and $\eta$ (eta)
is the **learning rate**: how big a step to take. Too small and training takes forever; too big and you jump
right over the valley. Repeat this thousands of times and the loss goes down:

![The loss dropping from about 0.5 to under 0.01 over 3000 training steps](/img/software/vision/ml/3-loss.webp)

### 3. Share out the blame: backpropagation

How do we find the slope for every weight, even ones buried deep in the network? We work backwards from the
answer. The output was too high by some amount; each neuron in the last layer contributed to that error in
proportion to its weight, so it gets that share of the blame. Those neurons pass their blame back to the
layer before them in the same way, and so on, all the way to the first layer. This is **backpropagation**,
and it is really just the chain rule from calculus applied over and over. It lets a computer find all
3 million slopes in about the same time it takes to run the network once.

## How a network sees a picture

A 640 × 640 color picture is 1.2 million numbers. Connecting every one of them to every neuron would need an
enormous number of weights, and it would ignore something important: **what's next to a pixel matters more
than what's far away**, and a ball is a ball whether it's in the top left or the bottom right.

So picture networks use **convolution**. A convolution slides a small grid of weights (a **filter**, often
3 × 3) across the picture. At every position, it multiplies the 9 pixels under it by the 9 weights and adds
them up, giving one output pixel. The same 9 weights are used everywhere in the picture.

Different weights find different things. Here are three filters, written by hand, run on a picture of an
AprilTag:

![Three 3 by 3 filters and what they do: one lights up vertical edges, one lights up horizontal edges, and one blurs the picture](/img/software/vision/ml/4-convolution.webp)

The vertical edge filter is negative on the left and positive on the right, so it gives a big answer
wherever the picture gets brighter from left to right: a vertical edge. Flat areas add up to 0.

A **convolutional neural network (CNN)** is layers of convolutions with ReLU in between, and here's the key
idea: **the filter weights are learned**, exactly like the weights in the small network above. Nobody tells
the network to look for edges. It works out, during training, that the first layer needs edge and color
filters. The next layers combine those into curves, corners and textures; layers after that into parts of
objects (a round yellow highlight, the shadow under a ball); and the last layers into whole objects. Each
layer also shrinks the picture, so deeper layers each "see" a bigger patch of the original.

## Data is everything

A network only knows what its training examples taught it. Most of the work in a real project is the data:

- **Labeling.** Someone draws a box around every game piece in thousands of pictures and says what it is. We
  do this in [Roboflow](https://app.roboflow.com/4143-marswars).
- **Variety.** Pictures from different fields, lighting, distances, angles, with pieces touching, half
  hidden and blurred, and pictures with **no** pieces in them so the network learns what isn't one.
- **Augmentation.** During training, each picture is randomly flipped, cropped, brightened, darkened or
  blurred, so one real picture teaches the network about many situations.
- **Training, validation and test sets.** The pictures are split up. The network learns only from the
  **training** set. The **validation** set, which it never trains on, shows whether it is really learning or
  just memorizing. A network that does great on its training pictures but badly on new ones is
  **overfitting**: it memorized the answers instead of learning the idea, like a student who memorized last
  year's test.
