# Deep Dive: How PnP Finds the Camera

The [tag detector](./detector-deep-dive.mdx) gives us each tag's ID and the pixel positions of its four
corners. The [field layout](./camera-setup.mdx#field-layout) tells us where each of those corners really is
on the field, in meters. **PnP** (Perspective-n-Point) answers the question:

> Where must the camera be, and which way must it be pointing, for those field corners to show up at
> exactly those pixels?

This page builds up the math behind that question step by step, and then explains how the method we use,
**SQPnP**, finds the answer. It uses some algebra, trigonometry and a few matrices; if you haven't seen
matrices yet, read them as "a compact way to write several equations at once" and you'll be fine.

## The pinhole camera

Every camera can be pictured as a box with a tiny hole in the front. Light from a point in the world goes
in a straight line through the hole and lands on the sensor at the back. A real lens gathers much more light
than a pinhole, but it sends the light to (nearly) the same place.

![Side view of a pinhole camera: a ray from a tag corner passes through the pinhole and hits the sensor at height u](/img/software/vision/pnp/1-pinhole.webp)

The two triangles in the drawing are **similar**: same angles, different sizes. So their sides are in
proportion:

$$
\frac{u}{f} = \frac{X}{Z}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
u = f\,\frac{X}{Z}
$$

- $X$ is how far the point is to the side of the camera, and $Z$ is how far in front of it, both in meters.
- $f$ is the **focal length**: the distance from the pinhole to the sensor, measured in pixels.
- $u$ is where the point lands on the sensor, in pixels from the center.

This one equation explains a lot. Twice as far away ($Z$ doubled) means half the size in the picture. And
dividing by $Z$ is what makes far-away things smaller: **perspective**.

The same is true vertically, and pixel numbers start in the top left corner of the picture, not the middle,
so we add the position of the picture's center $(c_x, c_y)$:

$$
u = f_x \frac{X}{Z} + c_x
\qquad
v = f_y \frac{Y}{Z} + c_y
$$

The four numbers $f_x, f_y, c_x, c_y$ are the camera's **intrinsics**: they describe the camera itself, not
where it is. They are what [calibration](./camera-setup.mdx#calibration) measures, along with five more
numbers that describe how the lens bends straight lines (**distortion**). Written as a matrix, the
intrinsics are:

$$
K = \begin{bmatrix} f_x & 0 & c_x \\ 0 & f_y & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

## Where the camera is: R and t

The equations above need the point's position **relative to the camera** ($X, Y, Z$ measured from the
camera, with $Z$ straight out of the lens). But the field layout gives each corner's position **on the
field**. To go from one to the other, we need the camera's pose:

- a **rotation** $R$: which way the camera is pointing. It is a 3 × 3 matrix; multiplying a point by it turns
  the point around the origin.
- a **translation** $t$: where the camera is. It is a list of 3 numbers in meters.

A corner at field position $P$ is at $R\,P + t$ as seen from the camera. Putting it all together, the pixel
where a field point appears is:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
\sim
K \,\big( R\,P + t \big)
$$

The $\sim$ means "equal after dividing by the last entry", which is the divide-by-$Z$ from the pinhole.

**PnP runs this backwards.** We know $K$ (from calibration), we know the field positions $P_i$ of the
corners (from the field layout), and we measured their pixels $(u_i, v_i)$ (from the detector). We want $R$
and $t$. That is 6 unknown numbers: 3 for where the camera is, 3 for which way it's pointing.

Each corner gives 2 equations (one for $u$, one for $v$), so 3 corners would be just enough in theory, and
one tag has 4. With several tags, we have many more equations than unknowns.

## Reprojection error: what "best" means

Real measurements are never perfect. The detector's corners are off by a fraction of a pixel, the tags might
be a few millimeters from where the field drawing says, and the calibration isn't perfect either. So no
single pose makes every corner land *exactly* on its pixel. Instead we look for the pose that is **closest**.

Pick any guess for $R$ and $t$, and use the camera equation to work out where each corner *should* appear.
The distance between where it should appear and where the detector actually found it is that corner's
**reprojection error**:

![The corners the detector found (black) and where a wrong guess for the camera pose says they should be (red). The arrows are the reprojection errors.](/img/software/vision/pnp/2-reprojection.webp)

PnP finds the pose that makes the total of the squared errors as small as possible:

$$
E(R, t) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big\| \, (u_i, v_i) - \text{project}(R\,P_i + t) \, \big\|^2
$$

This is the same idea as a line of best fit (least squares), but instead of choosing a slope and an
intercept, we're choosing a camera position and direction.

## Why this is hard

For a line of best fit, the error is a simple bowl-shaped function of the slope and intercept, so there is
one lowest point and a formula that finds it. PnP is harder for two reasons:

1. **The divide-by-$Z$** makes the error a curved, lumpy function of the pose. It can have several valleys,
   and a solver that just walks downhill from a starting guess can get stuck in the wrong one.
2. **Not every 3 × 3 matrix is a rotation.** A rotation has to keep lengths and angles the same, which means
   its three columns must each have length 1 and be at right angles to each other ($R^\mathsf{T} R = I$), and
   it can't mirror the world ($\det R = +1$). The solver has to search only among real rotations.

Older methods either solve with only a few points, or start from a rough guess and improve it step by step
and hope they end up in the right valley. Our detector uses **SQPnP**
([Terzakis and Lourakis, 2020](https://www.ecva.net/papers/eccv_2020/papers_ECCV/papers/123460460.pdf)),
which is designed to find the best answer every time.

## How SQPnP works

SQPnP stands for **Sequential Quadratic Programming PnP**. Here are its ideas, in order.

**1. Measure the error in 3D instead.** Every pixel the detector found defines a **line of sight**: the
straight line from the camera through that pixel, out into the world. If the pose were perfect, each corner
would sit exactly on its line of sight. Instead of pixels, SQPnP measures error in 3D: roughly, how far each corner
(moved by the guessed $R$ and $t$) is from its line of sight. This 3D distance behaves much like the pixel error, but without the
divide-by-$Z$, so the math stays simpler.

**2. Get rid of $t$.** For any given rotation, the best translation can be worked out directly with a
formula (it's a linear least-squares problem, like a line of best fit). Substituting that formula back in
leaves an error that depends on the rotation alone.

**3. The error is now a simple quadratic.** Write the 9 numbers of $R$ as one list $r$. The error becomes

$$
E(r) = r^\mathsf{T}\, \Omega\, r
$$

where $\Omega$ is a 9 × 9 matrix built once from the corners and pixels. A quadratic like this is the
higher-dimensional version of a parabola $y = a x^2$: very easy to minimize on its own. The catch is that
$r$ must still be a real rotation (from "why this is hard" above).

**4. Search carefully among rotations.** "Minimize a quadratic, subject to some equations that must stay
true" is a classic problem called **quadratic programming**. SQPnP solves it with **sequential quadratic
programming**: from a starting rotation, it takes a step that lowers the error while bending as little as
possible away from the set of rotations, then snaps back onto a true rotation, and repeats. It usually
settles in a handful of steps.

**5. Start in several places.** To avoid getting stuck in the wrong valley, SQPnP runs that search from
several starting rotations, built from the eigenvectors of $\Omega$ (the directions in which the error grows
the slowest). The authors proved that
with these starting points, one of the searches always ends at the true lowest point. SQPnP keeps the
best answer it finds.

The result is the camera's rotation $R$ and translation $t$.

## From the camera's pose to the robot's pose

PnP answers "where is the field, seen from the camera". We want the opposite, "where is the camera on the
field", so the detector inverts the transform: the camera's position on the field is $-R^\mathsf{T} t$, and
its rotation is $R^\mathsf{T}$.

Then the [proxy client](./pipeline.mdx#4-sending-it-to-the-roborio) moves that pose from the camera to the
center of the robot, using where the camera is mounted (the
[camera pose](./camera-setup.mdx#camera-pose) in its pipeline), and sends the robot's x and y (meters) and
heading (radians) to the roboRIO.

## Why we use many tags at once

Our detector puts the corners of **every tag the camera sees** into one PnP solve. Here's why. These are 500
simulated pictures of the same scene, solved with SQPnP, with each detected corner off by about half a pixel
(a realistic amount):

![With one tag, the camera position estimates spread over more than a meter. With four tags, they are all within about a centimeter.](/img/software/vision/pnp/3-one-vs-many.webp)

With **one tag** about 3.5 m away, the left/right estimate spreads over more than a meter and the heading
by about 0.17 rad (about 10°). With **four tags**, the spread drops to under a centimeter and about
0.002 rad.

The reason is that a single tag is small and flat. From far away, a tag turned slightly to the left looks
almost exactly like one turned slightly to the right; the difference is less than a pixel. Half a pixel of
noise is enough to flip the answer, and since the camera's position is worked out from the tag's angle, a
small angle error 3.5 m away becomes a big position error. This is called the **pose ambiguity** problem.
Corners from several tags, spread across the picture and at different distances, pin the pose down from
many directions at once, and the ambiguity disappears.

That's why, on the robot, a solution from several tags deserves more trust than one from a single tag (see
[Using Vision in Robot Code](./robot-code.mdx#standard-deviations-how-much-to-trust-the-camera)).
