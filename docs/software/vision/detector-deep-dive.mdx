# Deep Dive: How the Tag Detector Works

This page is for anyone who wants to know what actually happens inside the AprilTag detector on our
[Jetson](../hardware/jetsons.mdx). You don't need it to *use* vision on the robot (that's
[How Our Vision System Works](./pipeline.mdx)), but it's a great look at how a computer can "see".

Our detector is a GPU version of the [AprilTag 3](https://april.eecs.umich.edu/software/apriltag) algorithm
from the University of Michigan, rewritten for NVIDIA GPUs by FRC Team 971 and now part of
[Aquila Vision](https://github.com/coalman321/Aquila-Vision) (`perception/apriltag_detector`). Every picture
from every camera goes through the same eight steps.

The pictures on this page come from running each step on a made-up camera picture of tag 7, so you can
see what the detector "sees" at each stage.

![A camera picture with tag 7 in it, seen at an angle, with uneven lighting](/img/software/vision/detector/1-camera.webp)

## What makes a tag easy to find

An AprilTag is designed to be easy for a computer to find and hard to confuse with anything else:

- A **thick black square border**, surrounded by a **white margin**. A dark square inside a light square is
  a strong, simple shape to look for.
- Inside the border, a **6 × 6 grid of black and white cells**. Each cell is one **bit** (white = 1,
  black = 0), so a tag holds 36 bits. That pattern is the tag's ID.

FRC uses the **tag36h11** family: 36 bits, chosen so that any two tags in the family differ in at least
**11** of those bits. We'll see why that matters in step 8.

## Step 1: Make the picture smaller (decimation)

The detector first keeps only every other pixel in each direction (the setting is called
`quad_decimate = 2`). A 1280 × 960 picture becomes 640 × 480: a quarter of the pixels, so every step after
this is about four times faster.

Finding the *outline* of a tag doesn't need every pixel. The full-size picture comes back in step 7 to place
the corners as precisely as possible.

## Step 2: Black, white, or "not sure" (adaptive thresholding)

Next, every pixel is sorted into **black** or **white**. The obvious way is to pick one brightness, say
128 out of 255, and call everything darker "black". That fails as soon as the lighting isn't even, and on a
real field it never is:

![Left: one threshold for the whole picture turns the darker half of the wall black and merges it with the tag. Right: a threshold per tile keeps the tag's border separate.](/img/software/vision/detector/3-threshold.webp)

Instead, the detector uses a different threshold for each part of the picture:

1. Cut the picture into **tiles of 4 × 4 pixels**, and find the darkest (min) and brightest (max) pixel in
   each tile.
2. Spread each tile's min and max to its neighbors, so each tile uses the darkest and brightest values in
   the **3 × 3 block of tiles** around it (12 × 12 pixels).
3. If max − min is tiny (less than 5 out of 255), the area is flat: no edge here. The pixel is marked
   **"not sure"** (the gray in the picture) and ignored from then on.
4. Otherwise, the pixel is **white** if it is brighter than the halfway point $\frac{\min + \max}{2}$, and
   **black** if it is darker.

A black-and-white edge in a dim corner and the same edge in a bright corner both end up crisp, because each
one is compared with its own neighborhood. The flat wall comes out as noise, which is fine: the following
steps throw away anything that doesn't look like a tag.

## Step 3: Group touching pixels (connected components)

Now the detector groups pixels into **blobs**: a blob is a set of same-colored pixels that touch each other.
Every blob gets its own ID number. In the picture below, every blob has a random color:

![Every connected black or white region gets its own color](/img/software/vision/detector/4-blobs.webp)

Look at the tag: its black border is one big blob, the white margin around it is another, and each white
patch inside is its own blob.

The detector uses an algorithm called **union-find** (a GPU version by Allegretti et al., 2019). Each pixel
starts in its own group; whenever two touching pixels have the same color, their groups are merged. On a
GPU, thousands of pixels are checked at the same time, which is why this takes a fraction of a millisecond.

## Step 4: Find the edges between blobs

A tag's outline is where a black blob meets a white blob. For every pixel, the detector checks its neighbors
to the right and below (including the diagonals). Each time a black pixel touches a white one, it records a
**boundary point** halfway between them, labeled with **which pair of blobs** it separates.

Then all the points are sorted by their blob pair, so each pair's edge is one list. Most pairs are tiny
specks of noise, so the detector keeps only pairs whose edge has **at least 24 points** and that cover a
reasonable area. It also checks which side the black is on, so it only keeps edges with the right
"dark inside, light outside" shape.

![Left: every black/white edge in the picture. Right: only the edges big enough to be a tag are left.](/img/software/vision/detector/5-boundaries.webp)

## Step 5: Find the four corners

Each remaining edge is a list of points that might go around a square. To find out, the detector:

1. Finds the center of the points and **sorts them by angle** around it, so they go around the shape in
   order.
2. For each point, fits a straight line through it and its neighbors on both sides, and measures how badly
   the line fits (the **line-fit error**).

Along a straight side, the neighbors lie on a line and the error is almost zero. At a corner, half the
neighbors are on one side and half on the next, so no single line fits them and the error jumps. **The four
biggest peaks are the corners:**

![The line-fit error around the tag's edge: four sharp peaks, one at each corner](/img/software/vision/detector/6-corner-peaks.webp)

How is "how badly a line fits" measured? For a set of points, the best-fitting line goes through their
average point. How spread out the points are *across* that line is the smallest eigenvalue of their
covariance matrix, a 2 × 2 matrix built from sums of $x^2$, $y^2$ and $xy$. The detector keeps running
totals of those sums so it can work out the fit for any group of neighbors with a few subtractions,
instead of adding up every point again.

## Step 6: Fit the four sides

With the corners known, the points between each pair of corners are one side. The detector fits the best
straight line to each side's points (least squares, the same "line of best fit" as in math class) and finds
where neighboring lines cross. Those four crossings are the corners of a **quad**:

![The four sides' points in four colors, the fitted quad in yellow and its corners circled](/img/software/vision/detector/7-quad.webp)

Using lines fitted through dozens of points gives far better corners than just picking the corner pixels,
because the small errors in each point average out.

Quads that can't be a tag are thrown away: ones that aren't convex, are too small, or have corners too sharp
or too flat.

## Step 7: Sharpen the edges on the full picture

The quad came from the half-size picture. Now the detector goes back to the **full-resolution** picture and
improves each side: it walks along the side, looks across it for the spot where the brightness changes
fastest (the true edge), and fits a new line through those spots.

This is also where the camera's lens comes in. Real lenses bend straight lines a little, especially near the
edges of the picture. Our version of this step uses the camera's [calibration](./camera-setup.mdx#calibration)
to undo that bending while it fits the lines, so the corners are where a perfect camera would have seen
them.

## Step 8: Read the code (decoding)

Now we know where the tag is, but not which tag it is. A tag seen at an angle is squished into a
non-square shape, so the detector computes a **homography**: a formula that maps any point on a flat,
square tag to where it appears in the picture. With it, the detector can find the middle of every cell of
the tag's grid, however tilted the tag is:

![The tag's grid drawn on the picture, with the bit read from the middle of each of the 36 cells](/img/software/vision/detector/8-decode.webp)

For each of the 36 data cells, it samples the brightness at the cell's center and compares it with the
brightness of the black border and the white margin nearby: closer to white is a 1, closer to black is a 0.
That gives a 36-bit number.

Then it looks that number up in the list of all **587** tag36h11 codes, trying all four rotations (the
detector doesn't know which way is up yet). The comparison counts the **Hamming distance**: how many bits
are different.

- For the picture above, the best match is **tag 7 with 0 bits different**. The next best tag in the whole
  family is **11 bits** away, exactly as the family promises.
- Our detector accepts a match with **up to 1 wrong bit** (`bits_corrected = 1`). A smudge, a glare spot or
  a person's hand over one cell still reads correctly.
- Because every code is at least 11 bits from every other, it would take many wrong bits to turn one tag
  into another. Accepting more wrong bits would find damaged tags more often, but it would also make it more
  likely that random patterns (a poster, a bumper number) get read as a tag. One bit keeps false tags very
  rare.

Whichever rotation matched tells the detector which corner is the tag's top left, so the four corners are
reported in a fixed order. The detector's output for each tag is its **ID** and its **four corners in
pixels**. Turning those corners into a position on the field is the job of
[PnP](./pnp-deep-dive.mdx).

## Why use a GPU?

A GPU (graphics processing unit) has thousands of small cores that all run the same instructions on
different data at the same time. Steps 1 to 6 do the same small job for every pixel or every point, which
is exactly what a GPU is good at: the Jetson works on a whole picture at once instead of one pixel after
another. Decoding (step 8) runs on the Jetson's CPU, spread over 6 threads, since by then there are only a
handful of quads left.

That speed is what lets one Jetson keep up with several cameras at high frame rates, and it means the
picture is as fresh as possible when its pose reaches the robot.
